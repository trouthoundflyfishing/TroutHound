Fly Tying

Tools, materials, and the core techniques to build trout flies that hold up and catch fish.

Basic Tools

You can start with a modest kit. Quality tools last for years.

ToolPurpose
ViseHolds the hook firmly while you work
BobbinHolds and tensions the tying thread
ScissorsFine points for clean cuts (dedicated tying scissors)
Hackle pliersGrip and wrap delicate hackle feathers
Bodkin / needleApply cement, pick out dubbing, clear eyes
Whip-finish toolMakes a secure finishing knot (or learn by hand)
Hair stackerEven the tips of deer/elk hair (useful later)
Starter tip: A simple rotary or non-rotary vise, a good bobbin, sharp scissors, and a whip-finish tool will get you through dozens of classic patterns.

Core Materials

Hooks

  • Dry fly: lightweight, standard or 1XL
  • Nymph: 2XL–3XL, often with a heavier wire
  • Streamer: longer shank (3XL–4XL)
  • Sizes 12–16 cover most trout situations; keep 8–10 and 18–20 on hand too

Thread

  • 6/0 or 8/0 for most trout flies
  • Color to match or contrast the body as the pattern dictates

Common Materials

  • Dubbing (hare’s ear, squirrel, synthetic blends)
  • Hackle (dry-fly quality rooster, soft hen for wet flies)
  • Pheasant tail fibers, peacock herl
  • Marabou, rabbit strips, chenille (streamers)
  • Wire (copper, gold, silver) for ribbing and weight
  • Beads (tungsten or brass) for nymphs
  • Head cement or UV resin for durability

Fundamental Techniques

1. Secure the Hook & Start the Thread

Clamp the hook firmly. Lay thread on the shank and wrap rearward in touching turns to build a smooth base. Leave the tag and cover it.

2. Build the Body

Dubbing: twist a thin amount onto the thread and wrap. Herl or fibers: tie in at the rear and wrap forward. Keep bulk under control so proportions stay correct.

3. Ribbing

Tie in wire or tinsel at the rear, wrap the body, then spiral the rib forward. It reinforces the body and adds segmentation.

4. Hackle

For dry flies, tie in a quality hackle by the tip or butt and wrap 2–4 turns. For soft-hackles, fewer turns of a softer feather.

5. Head & Finish

Build a small neat head, whip-finish, and apply a drop of cement. Clear the eye with a bodkin before the cement sets.

Proportions matter: Body length, hackle size, and tail length should look balanced. Study a finished example of the pattern before you start.

A Sensible Learning Path

  1. Woolly Bugger — simple, forgiving, and catches everything.
  2. Pheasant Tail Nymph — teaches herl, wire rib, and legs.
  3. Hare’s Ear — dubbing and a rough, buggy look.
  4. Adams or Elk Hair Caddis — dry-fly hackle and wing.
  5. Then move to soft-hackles, bead-head variants, and streamers.

Tie multiples of the same pattern. Consistency improves faster than jumping between many different flies.

Durability Tips

  • Use enough thread tension — loose wraps fail first.
  • Rib fragile herl bodies with fine wire.
  • Cement the head and any critical wraps.
  • Don’t overcrowd the hook eye; leave room for the tippet knot.
